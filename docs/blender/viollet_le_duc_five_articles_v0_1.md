### What this cycle bought us, in order of value to the factory

Five readers were sent into Viollet-le-Duc's *Dictionnaire raisonné de l'architecture française du XIe au XVIe siècle* (Paris, B. Bance / A. Morel, 1854-1868) on fr.wikisource. Four delivered: **Charpente** (vol. 3, pp. 1-58), **Construction — Voûtes** (vol. 4, pp. 62-126, with the companion article *Voûte*, vol. 9), **Maison** (vol. 6, pp. 214-300) and **Escalier** (vol. 5, pp. 287-331). **The fifth, Serrurerie, never wrote its findings file** — see the section on it below; the raw text and all 54 plates are on disk and two verifiers did enough source work that the article is half-mined, but nothing was delivered by the reader itself.

Ranked by what a generator can actually do with it:

1. **A complete vault-web coursing algorithm, with two regional presets.** Viollet states the French rule as an executable procedure (divide the half wall-arch extrados by the rubble width to get *n*; divide the diagonal into the same *n*; join corresponding points; keep every joint arris in a vertical plane) and the Anglo-Norman rule as its opposite (course off the diagonals outward, interlacing at the ridge). Both are VERIFIED on plates. Critically, he proves on one plate (fig. 65, VERIFIED) that **the same rib skeleton carries either web**, so `rib_layout` and `web_coursing_rule` must be independent fields in our schema.
2. **A fully dimensioned French cathedral truss, independently confirmed against its own plate.** Reims: base 14.40 m, rise 15.50 m, rafter 17.00 m, max scantling 0.22 m (= 8 pouces), no ironwork at all. Verification decoded the plate and recovered the graduated 5 m bar at 58.8 px/m, giving half-base 7.19 m, rise 15.49 m, rafter 17.08 m, poinçon 0.221 m — every stated number reproduced from the drawing. This is our hardest calibration point in any source.
3. **The stair layer, which we had nothing on at all.** A 2×2×2 type taxonomy plus five further named types, ~40 stated dimensions, three hard dating boundaries (c.1200, c.1275, c.1400), and stated reasons for nearly every form.
4. **Named French vocabulary, ~360 deduplicated terms with English glosses** (`vocabulary.tsv`). This is the most reusable artefact of the cycle and includes the false friends that wreck translation — *ogive* never means "pointed arch"; *pêle* is a bolt; *étampe* is a swage die, not tinning; *laminer* is rolling, not lamination.
5. **Stated causes, which are worth more than shapes**, because they tell a generator what must stay true: pitch driven by wall-head width and scantling and covering; the tas-de-charge derived from rib-bundle plan geometry; the jetty caused by land economics inside a fixed wall circuit; the newel widened to stop treads going knife-edged; steps as *étrésillons* bracing newel to cage.
6. **Six cross-source disagreements that change our algorithms**, and — after verification — three claimed disagreements that dissolved into agreements, which are more valuable still.

### Charpente (vol. 3, pp. 1-58) — 48 plates, 29 VERIFIED

**The system, named.** *Charpentes à chevrons portant fermes*: between the principal trusses, every common rafter is itself framed almost like a truss, standing on *patins*/*blochets* in the double wall-plates, with no tie at its base. Verbatim: «on composa les combles de fermes entre lesquelles vinrent se ranger des chevrons à peu près armés comme elles, affleurant le plan passant par la face externe des arbalétriers, et ne différant guère des fermes-maîtresses que parce qu'ils n'avaient point d'entraits à leur base, mais reposaient seulement sur des patins assemblés dans les doubles sablières» — "the roofs were composed of trusses between which were ranged common rafters armed almost like them, flush with the plane passing through the outer face of the principal rafters, and differing from the principal trusses hardly at all except that they had no tie-beams at their base but rested only on sole-pieces assembled in the double wall-plates." For the generator this collapses "truss" and "common rafter" into one parameterised family and abolishes purlins in the classic case.

**Why, stated as a masonry argument.** «au lieu de reporter la charge de tout le comble et de sa couverture sur les maîtresses-fermes […] il répartissait également les pesanteurs sur la totalité de la tête des murs ou bahuts» — "instead of carrying the load of the whole roof and its covering onto the principal trusses, it distributed the weights equally over the whole head of the walls or dwarf walls." So roof system is *determined by* the masonry below: thin piers plus flyers force even loading, thick walls permit principals and purlins.

**The tie doctrine, and it is a doctrine, not a survey finding.** «l'entrait, qui doit être uniquement d'empêcher l'écartement des arbalétriers, mais qui ne peut et ne doit supporter aucune charge» — "the tie-beam, whose function must be solely to prevent the principal rafters from spreading, and which cannot and must not carry any load." Note the normative *doit*. Verification caught the reader recording this as an observed property of the Reims truss when the Reims text contradicts it: «deux cours de pannes portées par une contre-fiche G posée sous l'arbalétrier et venant s'assembler dans l'entrait et à la tête d'un poteau H» — the one member carrying the whole lower roof load is footed **in** the tie. Keep TIE_MODE as the switch (see cross-source below); do not encode "nothing touches the tie" as an invariant.

**PITCH — REFUTED AND REPLACED.** The reader's headline was a France-vs-England pitch split ("England 40-53°, France 57-68°", sold as a region discriminator). Verification killed it three ways. (a) Viollet says the opposite: «Excepté dans les provinces méridionales, où les charpentes conservèrent une faible inclinaison, partout en France et en Angleterre, on modifia, à la fin du XIIe siècle, le système des charpentes apparentes» — "Except in the southern provinces, where roofs kept a shallow pitch, **everywhere in France and in England** the system of exposed roofs was modified at the end of the 12th century." The one low-pitch exception is intra-French (the Midi); England is grouped *with* France. (b) The reader's own VERIFIED fig. 31 is Ely — an English late-14th-c roof — and it measures **63°**, ten degrees above the claimed English ceiling; it was the one verified plate for which the reader recorded no pitch. (c) The reader's own VERIFIED French plates measure 39°, 41°, 42° and 50°, below the claimed French floor.

What Viollet actually states, and what to encode:
- «on la porta de 40 ou 50 degrés à 60 et même 65 degrés» — raised from 40 or 50° to 60 and even 65°.
- 45-50° for the failed trusses over Burgundian pointed barrels (Autun, Beaune, Saulieu named): «les arbalétriers, n'ayant guère qu'une inclinaison de 45 à 50 degrés».
- **Two causes at two dates, not one.** Mid-12th c: «employant des bois moins gros, il fallait donner aux arbalétriers une plus grande inclinaison, afin qu'ils ne fléchissent pas sous le poids de la couverture» — "using smaller timbers, it became necessary to give the principal rafters a greater inclination so that they would not bend under the weight of the covering." 13th c: the *bahut* has shrunk to «un mètre environ» and the rafter foot plus purlin depth no longer fits on it.
- Covering caps pitch: *tuile romaine* implies a near-flat roof; a steep Romanesque gable implies *grandes tuiles plates*.
- Jambette rake follows pitch: «plus les combles sont aigus, plus les jambettes se rapprochent de la verticale» — raked ones «retiennent mieux la poussée».

**Corrected generator rule:** `pitch = f(date, available wall-head width, timber scantling, covering)` — and **not** a France/England discriminator. Evidence band from this article alone: pre-1150 France 39-50° (figs 1, 2, 5 measured); 13th-c French light apparent roofs 50-57° (fig. 25 = 50, fig. 21 = 57); 13th-16th-c French great roofs 59-68° (fig. 9 = 61, fig. 12 = 62, fig. 23 = 64, Reims = 65.1 by arithmetic, fig. 34 bis = 66, fig. 26 = 68); England 53-63° (Westminster = 53.1 by arithmetic, fig. 29 = 53, Ely fig. 31 = 63 measured). **The ranges overlap heavily.** What does discriminate region here is TIE_MODE, scantling size, and whether purlins are used.

**Purlin position is the four-state dating enum**, and each state changes the eaves geometry and the required wall thickness: on top of the rafter (antique/Romanesque) → tenoned into the rafter, laid flat below its outer face (short-lived Guienne experiment, judged weak) → abolished entirely (13th-c *chevrons portant fermes*) → reintroduced **under** the rafter with gussets (late 15th c). Invariant for the whole French Gothic family: the *arbalétrier*'s outer face is coplanar with the chevrons; nothing projects outside that plane.

**Wind, with the period term.** «On appelle hiement, en termes de charpenterie, le mouvement que l'effort du vent imprime aux fermes et chevrons» — a quotable definition of the failure mode. Devices, in his order: longitudinal *croix de Saint-André* between *entretoises*; *liens* into the axial entretoises and the king-posts; *goussets* into the purlins; and crossed *écharpes* lapped *à tiers-bois* on the **outside** of the rafters in the roof plane. Note the reader dropped the *liens* from the Reims longitudinal node: the text gives «au moyen de liens **et** de croix de Saint-André».

**No ironwork.** «Le charpentier du moyen âge n'appelle pas à son aide le serrurier […] On ne voit, dans toute cette charpente, aucune ferrure» — a hard asset rule. Fastening vocabulary is limited to oak pegs (*chevilles*), wooden keys (*clefs de bois*) in *moises*, dovetails and housed tenons. Iron is a *dating* signal when it does appear (Saint-Ouen's square-headed *chevillettes*; bolts with *clavettes* in 15th-c armed beams).

**Shape rules keyed to force type**, which is what makes generated frames look structurally literate: chamfers stop short of every joint «pour laisser toute la force du bois là où un tenon vient s'assembler dans une mortaise»; tension members are waisted (*élégies*) between their keys «car ces bois n'agissant que comme suspension»; compression members keep full section and gain *renforts* left standing in the solid «sans affamer le bois».

**Documented failure and retrofit** — a legitimate "repaired" asset variant: Hargnies (Nord), late 15th c, built tie-less, «a poussé au vide», and about mid-16th c «des tirants furent posés de deux en deux fermes sous les arbalétriers».

**The sheathing is structural**, stated twice independently: at Notre-Dame «quand on enlève la volige, on fait remuer à la main les fermes-maîtresses»; at Hargnies «on se fiait, avec assez de raison, aux bardeaux pour maintenir les courbes légères du chevronnage, ces bardeaux formant comme une voûte». If we ever simulate these frames the boarding must be in the model.

**Corrections verification forced on the Reims parametric account** (`parametric_ferme`): the CHEVRON bracing (jambettes N, collars O) must not be counted into the truss part list — the text explicitly contrasts the two systems, and measurement puts the O collars at 2.66 m and 8.87 m along the rafter against the ferme's P and R at 3.39 m and 6.09 m, different levels in different planes. "Collars at equal intervals" is false for this truss (intervals 3.39 / 2.70 / 10.99 m) and the «à intervalles égaux» phrase belongs to the Notre-Dame hip truss of fig. 9 alone — full text «La flexion des arbalétriers **de cette ferme** est donc arrêtée à intervalles égaux par les trois entraits retroussés». The *sous-arbalétriers-moises* are 2 **pairs** (4 cheeks), each unspliced and «pas moins de 14 m,50» long. Section C is on the poinçon axis, section D on the dotted line A-B — two different planes, 0.5-0.9 m apart. The 9-step `assembly_order` appears nowhere in the article and must be labelled inferred.

### Construction — Voûtes (vol. 4, pp. 62-126) — 52 plates, 33 figures VERIFIED

**The two-stage build, and what it is not.** «ils firent sortir de la voûte d'arête romaine ou byzantine le nerf noyé dans son épaisseur, le construisirent en matériaux appareillés, résistants, et le posèrent sur le cintre de charpente ; puis, au lieu de maçonner la voûte autour, ils la maçonnèrent par-dessus, considérant alors cet arc laissé saillant, en sous-œuvre, comme un cintre permanent» — "they drew out of the Roman or Byzantine groin vault the nerve buried in its thickness, built it in coursed resistant material, and laid it on the timber centre; then, instead of walling the vault around it, they walled it over, considering that arch, left projecting beneath, as a permanent centre." **Verification flags this as CONJECTURE**: it continues an invented speech Viollet puts in 12th-century architects' mouths, and the sentence immediately before it is his own disclaimer — «Que les architectes occidentaux aient fait ce raisonnement en plein XIIe siècle, nous ne l'affirmerons pas ; mais leurs monuments le font pour eux, et cela nous suffit» ("we shall not affirm it; but their monuments do it for them, and that suffices us"). Use the two-stage build model; do not cite the invention story as evidence.

**Construction order, VERIFIED at four separate places in the text and on the plates.**
1. Ribs on timber centering, then web over.
2. Epure order: divide the *formeret*/*doubleau* extrados first, then divide the diagonal to the **same count** — «on divise en nombres égaux l'extrados des arcs ogives […] et l'on procède de suite à la construction des voûtes sans couchis».
3. Springer stack: horizontal beds up to the level Q where the transverse arch frees itself from the diagonal, divided by the quarry bed height; only then radial cuts. «Ce point Q indique donc la hauteur où l'arc doubleau se dégage de l'arc ogive : c'est le niveau du lit du dernier sommier. Il s'agit de diviser la hauteur PQ en un certain nombre d'assises, suivant la hauteur des bancs.» So tas-de-charge height is *computed from plan geometry*, and the number of courses inside it is set by the *material*.
4. **The abacus is derived, not chosen** — Viollet's own parenthesis: «Le contour du lit inférieur du premier sommier obtenu, nous pouvons tracer (seulement alors) le tailloir du chapiteau». VERIFIED on fig. 48 ter, which shows three stacked one-piece springers on a foliate capital with the rib profiles progressively more separated on each higher bed. Dependency order for our pipeline: vault plan → springer bed outline → abacus → capital → pier section → buttress. Viollet uses the word himself: «La voûte étant désormais le générateur de toutes les parties des édifices voûtés».
5. Web order: «il monte **au moins** un tiers de chacun des côtés de son remplissage comme un mur […] Mais au delà du premier tiers, ou environ, il faut l'aide d'une cerce» — at least a third as plain walling, then the sliding template. VERIFIED on fig. 57. (The reader softened a lower bound into a central value; use ≥ 1/3.)

**Coursing direction, VERIFIED triple-attested.** French rule: «les remplissages des voûtes en arcs d'ogive sont fermés au moyen de rangs de moellons piqués, posés perpendiculairement (en projection horizontale) aux formerets, de telle sorte que ces rangs de moellons viennent se joindre parallèlement sur la ligne des clefs, ou ligne faîtière». Anglo-Norman rule, stated by contrast: «bandèrent les rangs de moellon, non point des formerets ou arcs doubleaux sur les arcs ogives, comme les constructeurs de l'Île-de-France, mais des arcs ogives aux formerets et arcs doubleaux, en les entre-croisant à la clef». A fourth independent confirmation at Mouliherne. VERIFIED on figs 55, 63, 64 and 65 (65 cropped and enlarged panel by panel).

**Correction to finding 4's headline:** Viollet divides the **half** formeret — «combien l'extrados du demi arc formeret AF, compris sa naissance verticale, contient de fois XX′ : soit quatre fois» — so *n*=4 is courses per half-cell. Taken as "the formeret", the rule halves course density.

**Why the joints must be vertical, which is the reason worth more than the shape.** «Ces lits à l'intrados traçant des courbes, il en résulte que la section CD se trouve avoir un plus grand développement que la section DB qui commande le nombre des rangées de moellon» — the intrados beds trace curves, so an intermediate section develops longer than the base section that fixed the course count; keeping the *cerce* vertical spreads the surplus over every course automatically. Consequence: **every French web course is a lens**, thicker at mid-length than at its ends — «ces rangs de moellons concaves à l'intrados et plus épais, par conséquent, au milieu du rang qu'aux deux extrémités». (Verification struck the reader's first supporting quote for this: «il eût fallu tailler chacun d'eux pour lui donner plus de largeur au milieu qu'aux extrémités» has *chacun d'eux* = «planches ou madriers», timber lagging boards, not stone courses. The claim stands on the third fragment.)

**Rule set that is directly implementable, all VERIFIED:**
- Oblong bay: `R(diagonal) = R(transverse) = 0.5 × bay diagonal`, two centres offset inward from each springing by that radius. Motive stated: identical voussoir templates.
- Square bay with intermediate transverse arch: hard inequality chain, `rise(intersecting doubleau) = R(ogive) > rise(principal doubleau) > rise(formeret incl. stilting)`.
- Stilting on radial plans: `stilt = rise_wide − semicircular_rise_narrow`, all abaci at one level. Viollet explicitly refutes "stilted arch = eastern influence".
- Rib section: strength follows **depth**, not width — keep depth, shrink width, blunt the outer arris for skew springings. Dated envelope table: square 0.22 m (8 pouces) for small vaults, 0.33 m (1 pied) and 0.50 m (18 pouces) as the vault widens, mid-12th c; diagonals narrow at the end of the century.
- n-gon hall → n triangular cells, one rib per vertex to the centre.
- Apse: last straight bay length = polygon side length, which is the condition making all radiating ribs equal and all crowns level.
- Named failure mode with a named fix (fig. 51 bis, VERIFIED): radiating ribs butting on the unabutted crown of a transverse arch can warp it; the drawn repair inserts two counter-branches.
- 16th-c abutment rule (fig. 32 bis, VERIFIED): trisect the arch, swing the chord, produce it, drop a vertical — worked out for a true semicircle it gives abutment thickness = span/4, decreasing as the arch sharpens, with its own guard condition «des pieds-droits […] qui n'ont pas plus d'une fois et demie le diamètre ou la base de ces arcs». Note: the span/4 result is the reader's derivation, not Viollet's statement, and it was flagged as needing re-verification.
- Regional web presets with the material reason for each: Île-de-France / Beauvaisis / Champagne = thin *cerce*-built rubble web, 2nd half 12th c; Burgundy = rendered rubble on lagging, «un expédient» not a method; Aquitaine / Anjou / Maine = domical web with cosmetic ribs; England = diagonal-referenced web with liernes and tiercerons.
- Real web thicknesses: 0.10-0.12 m for large late-12th-c Gothic vaults, and «Les remplissages des grandes voûtes de la cathédrale de Paris n'ont pas plus de 0,10 c. d'épaisseur» — the single most citable figure, against 0.40-0.50 m for a conventionally built Roman barrel.
- The *cerce* itself is a modellable tool (fig. 58, VERIFIED): two grooved boards about 0.04 m thick keyed together so the pair can be lengthened, hooked to the ribs by iron squares.
- Kit-of-parts vaults for the 16th c (figs 74 and 34, VERIFIED): four part types — transverse rib voussoir with lateral sockets, longitudinal flat-arch voussoir, pendant boss, rebated carved slab — on a regular rectangular grid. The most instanceable asset set in the article.

**Sens — REFUTED READING, and it fails in the direction opposite to the reader's guess.** The reader flagged «les rangs de moellons de ces remplissages sont posés parallèlement à la ligne Mg des clefs» as a possible domical outlier inside the royal domain. It is not. The sentence opens with the adversative *Cependant*, setting the actual coursing **against** the domical option just described, and Mg runs in plan from vault crown to formeret crown, so "parallel to Mg" *is* "perpendicular to the formeret in horizontal projection" — the same rule stated against a different reference line. Do not encode Sens as domical. Separately, the whole Sens *tracé* is a Viollet **restitution** and he says so: «nous n'établissons notre tracé que sur une hypothèse», then «voici qui vient encore appuyer notre restitution». The 12th-c semicircular formerets were destroyed and replaced in the late 13th c; the surviving evidence is only the colonnettes, formeret stubs, cornice and arcature — and he explicitly withdraws the coursing claim: «nous ne pouvons affirmer toutefois que les rangs de moellons de ces triangles aient été posés parallèlement à la ligne des clefs».

**Correction to fig. 64 (VERIFIED plate, wrong point identification).** Only C is a formeret crown: «réunir la clef du formeret C à la clef des arcs ogives D par un nerf saillant masquant la suture formée par la rencontre des triangles de remplissage en moellon ECD, FCD». E and F sit at the square's corners where the diagonals land. Only C-D is the suture masked by the ridge nerve. The substance — courses crossing at the ridges — is right.

### Maison (vol. 6, pp. 214-300) — 49 plates, 30 VERIFIED

**Root rule.** «Quant à la maison des villes, occupant un espace plus étroit par suite de la nécessité où l'on se trouvait d'enceindre ces villes de murailles, elle dut nécessairement abandonner […] les dispositions étendues à rez-de-chaussée pour superposer des étages» — a wall-bounded town converts plan area into storeys. Plot area, not fashion, sets storey count. And unlike the Roman house it looks **out**: «Les vues ne sont plus prises, ainsi que dans la maison antique, sur des cours intérieures, mais sur la voie publique, et la cour, s'il en existe, n'est réservée qu'aux services domestiques.» Glazing and ornament go on the street face; the court is a working yard.

**The programme repeats vertically; it does not accrete.** «D'ailleurs, pour la maison du bourgeois, le programme diffère peu. Toujours la salle à chaque étage avec escalier intérieur, ou, plus souvent, sur le derrière avec petite cour.» Per-storey occupancy: shop / family *salle*-bedroom / chambers / *galetas* for apprentices and servants.

**Roof orientation is derived, not chosen** — the kind of stated reason we want most. «Les ruelles d'isolement entre les maisons, qu'elles fussent simples ou jumelles, avaient nécessairement amené les architectes à élever les murs goutterots sur les ruelles et les pignons sur la rue» (alley plot ⇒ gable to street), and «généralement ce sont les pignons qui se présentent sur la rue, les terrains propres à bâtir ayant plus de profondeur que de largeur» with the explicit exception that a broad plot puts the gables on the party walls and makes the street front an eaves wall. Rule: ridge perpendicular to the street when depth > width, parallel when width > depth.

**Openings are derived from rooms, and asymmetry is correct output.** «La fenêtre n'est pas disposée avec un art symétrique, mais elle éclaire la pièce qu'elle est destinée à éclairer, et elle prend la dimension qui convient à cette pièce.» Plus the recipe for a convincing street: «certaines dispositions générales des plans étaient adoptées par tous au même moment […] on signale également dans les détails, dans la manière dont les jours sont percés, une extrême variété» — one shared plan template per province-and-date, high variance in openings and detail. Do not randomise the plan; randomise the elevation.

**Counter-intuitive and stated flatly:** medieval townsmen sought darkness. «les habitants cherchaient l'obscurité dans les intérieurs avec autant de soin que nous cherchons la lumière». Only from the 13th c is one well-lit room wanted, and the widest openings are in industrial towns because the house is also the workshop. Coupled pair: streets narrow as towns grow rich, windows enlarge in compensation — so wealth makes a town *darker* at street level.

**No building code.** «les règlements en vigueur concernant la plantation des maisons dans les villes de France au moyen âge, leurs saillies sur la voie publique, la manière de prendre les jours, les écoulements des eaux, variaient à l'infini, chaque seigneur ayant établi une coutume particulière». Model exactly those four things as per-town parameters; model fire risk as a consequence of the timber fraction, not as a regulation.

**The timber ladder is monotonic in date** — one number from the year gives the right building: upper storeys only → all but the ground floor → the whole face → finally boarded «comme un meuble, d'un bahut immense» with the nogging hidden behind panels (end 15th / early 16th c). Cause is land economics inside a fixed wall circuit, plus «rapidité dans l'exécution, économie, et, ce qui importait plus encore, faible surface occupée». Explicitly **not** geology: «Le plus ou moins d'abondance de l'une de ces deux matières […] n'avait pas une influence décisive sur le système de construction adopté» — which forbids the obvious procedural shortcut of deriving material from local stone availability.

**Window size has a structural reason in timber work**, and it is the kind of constraint that keeps a generator honest: «Au XVe siècle, les fenêtres de ces maisons de bois sont multipliées et petites ; cela était une nécessité de construction dès lors que les pans de bois atteignaient une grande hauteur» — tall frames move, so pane size must be inversely related to frame height. Two bracing devices with distinct jobs, one per storey: crosses in the *allèges* stop the jamb-posts leaning out, *décharges* carry each storey's weight to the corner posts.

**Structural boolean that is independent of material:** «Les poteaux corniers de face ne sont là que pour maintenir le pan de bois sur la rue, car derrière ces poteaux corniers s'élèvent les murs mitoyens en moellon portant cheminées» — at Laval and Reims the timber front is non-structural casing over masonry party walls; the 15th-c Rouen fronts are the structure. Drive `front_is_structural` from whether the party walls are masonry.

**Bastides — CORRECTED.** The reader claimed uniform houses licensing whole-town array instantiation. Half right. Viollet says identical **plans** with deliberately **varied elevations**, and the reader dropped the second half of the sentence: «Elles se composent toutes d'un rez-de-chaussée, d'un premier et quelquefois d'un second étage ; **leurs façades sont variées d'aspect, en raison des goûts ou de l'état de fortune de chacun**.» Instantiate the plan cell, randomise the façade per cell. The footnote qualifier also went missing: «Cette disposition est même conservée autour de la place, là où existe un portique : c'est une exception à la règle.» And the striking numbers — "at least twenty of these bastides due to the English domination", "perhaps fifty towns", "first as duke and soon as king" — are **Félix de Verneilh's**, quoted by Viollet, not Viollet's own; his own text credits Edward I only in the passing phrase «sous la domination d'Édouard Ier, dans la Guienne».

### Escalier (vol. 5, pp. 287-331) — 32 plates, all 32 VERIFIED

**The taxonomy, verbatim, and VERIFIED as the article's opening sentence.** «Nous distinguerons les escaliers extérieurs (qu'il ne faut pas confondre avec les perrons) des escaliers intérieurs, les escaliers à rampes droites des escaliers à girons et à vis, les escaliers de pierre des escaliers de bois.» It is a 2×2×2 product space — {exterior, interior} × {straight, winder/vis} × {stone, wood} — plus two further classes he treats separately: *escaliers de charpente et de menuiserie* (carpentry vs joinery) and the pivoting stair. "Interior" is defined functionally, not spatially: «desservant plusieurs étages d'un bâtiment, posés dans des cages comprises dans les constructions ou accolées à ces constructions» — so the great *vis hors-d'œuvre* is still an interior stair.

**THE SWORD-ARM QUESTION: it is folklore, and the negative is clean.** In 66,515 characters and 32 figures Viollet discusses handedness nowhere. Verification re-ran the counts independently: *dextr* 0, *senestr* 0, *épée* 0, *glaive* 0, *défens* 0, *assaill* 0, *attaqu* 0, *combat* 0, *ennemi* 0, *assiég* 0. *gauche* occurs exactly **once**, in the wedge-step footwork rule: «il fallait toujours poser le pied gauche sur la marche A, le pied droit sur la marche B en descendant, ou le contraire en montant». All ten *arme* hits are heraldic or Rabelais. The only defensive property he claims for a *vis* is instant barricading — advantage 7 of nine: «pour les châteaux, les tours, ils étaient barricadés en un moment». And his own plates disagree on hand: verification re-measured the arrows about independently fitted stair centres — fig. 7 tail 31° → head 70° (CCW), fig. 13 both arrows CCW, fig. 15 tail ~282° → head ~224° (CW, cross-checked against the dotted-tread convention). **Handedness stays a free parameter and must never be presented as a defensive requirement.** If we want a defensive stair, use the ones he documents: instant barricading, the pivoting stair that masks every door at a quarter turn, and the deliberate gap between fixed and rotating flights.

**The nine stated advantages of the vis** are the highest-value paragraph in the article because every item is an affordance or a constraint: embeddable or barely attached; takes little room; **doors may open at any point of the circumference and at any height**; easily lit; simple to build; **pitch is a free parameter**; instantly barricaded; can run full height; and can be crowned by a lantern giving onto a terrace.

**Comfort geometry, anthropometric.** «que chaque marche ait en largeur la longueur d'un pied d'homme, soit 0,28 c. à 0,30 c., et en hauteur de 0,15 c. à 0,20 c. au plus, ce qui donne une inclinaison de 22 degrés ou environ». Use the linear pair; the stated 22° does not follow (0.15/0.30 = 26.6°, 0.20/0.28 = 35.5°) and verification confirmed the arithmetic flag. 45° is explicitly a service/fortification expedient, «dangereuse ou fort pénible», and at 45° going equals rise by definition.

**The wedge-step trick with its stated reason** — a reason is worth more than a shape: «observant avec raison que l'on ne met jamais qu'un pied à la fois sur chaque marche […] il est inutile qu'une marche ait la largeur nécessaire à la pose du pied dans toute sa longueur». Recipe: alternating wedges, pair rise 0.30 m (so 0.15 m each), 0.30 m at the wide end, and a forced gait — which is a gameplay hook.

**Three hard dating boundaries, and they are the spine of a stair generator.**
- **Pre-1200:** ashlar newel drums each cut with a small *épaulement*, a helical rubble barrel (*berceau en spirale*) springing off the newel and the inner circular face, steps laid on its extrados with their arrises on the radii. Notching the vault bearings straight into the plain cylinder instead «affaiblit beaucoup celui-ci» — a good/bad quality axis, useful for ruin states.
- **c.1200:** the vault is abandoned. «lorsqu'au commencement du XIIIe siècle on exploita les pierres en plus grands morceaux qu'on ne l'avait fait jusqu'alors, on trouva plus simple de faire porter à chaque marche un morceau du noyau, de les faire mordre quelque peu l'une sur l'autre, et de leur ménager une portée entaillée de quelques centimètres le long du parement». One prefabricated step type, instanced. Motive stated as speed: «la nécessité d'arriver promptement à un résultat, de bâtir vite en un mot». And the treads are the bracing: «il avait encore l'avantage de relier le noyau avec la cage par toutes ces marches qui formaient autant d'étrésillons» — which is why a 0.23 m cage wall is documented.
- **c.1400:** the newel becomes one piece with helical mortises up the rake, in stone and in timber simultaneously.

**A geometric law with a stated reason:** «on élargit ces noyaux pour éviter l'aiguïté des marches se rapprochant du centre» — newel radius must grow with cage radius, because the constraint is a minimum tread width at the inner end. Then the big newels carry «des mains-courantes prises dans la masse et des saillies en forme de bandeau spirale, pour recevoir les petits bouts des marches» — two helices at one pitch on one shaft, handrail above, bandeau with step housings below (VERIFIED on fig. 20, labels A and B).

**Planning rules that are checkable predicates, not preferences.** Landings must be *de plain-pied* with the floors they serve; no flight may block a range of openings — «n'eussent jamais établi, dans un bâtiment, un escalier dont les rampes auraient bouché une ordonnance de baies, ainsi que cela se fait volontiers de notre temps». Four siting templates for a grand *vis*, each VERIFIED on a plate: entirely detached and joined only by a landing (Louvre); in the re-entrant angle of two porticoes, which yields **four** ground-floor doors plus one per upper level (Gaillon); independent in the middle of a wing (Blois); intercalated with the portico passing in front and the cage set back and square (Châteaudun). First stated reason for *hors-d'œuvre* is **wayfinding**: «Pour le rendre plus visible et plus aisé à trouver».

**Why castle stairs are many and small before the 15th c** — a programme rule, not a geometry rule: the great halls sat almost at ground level over a vaulted undercroft and were reached by wide *perrons*, so proper stairs served only the private apartments, and what mattered was putting each stair next to the rooms it served.

**Types that are pure gameplay.** The double-revolution *vis*, «de sorte que l'on pouvait descendre par l'une et remonter par l'autre sans se rencontrer et même sans se voir» — a stair on which two agents cannot meet. The concentric pair, where «La vis intérieure devenait escalier de service, et le degré circonvolutant, escalier d'honneur» — a ready-made access-control rule for NPC routing. The private two-storey *vis* in the wall thickness, documented socially by the Joinville anecdote of Louis IX and Marguerite de Provence at Pontoise, where the ushers beat the doors with their rods as a warning. And the **pivoting stair**, the only genuinely defensive stair in the article and a puzzle-lock rather than a sword-arm advantage: a quarter turn of the whole timber cylinder masks every door on every floor. Viollet's own licence for building trick stairs is unusually direct: «dans ces châteaux où les journées paraissaient fort longues, ces bizarreries, ces surprises, étaient autant de distractions à la vie monotone des châtelains et de leurs hôtes».

**Ornament budget, dated.** Pre-14th c: functional, asymmetric, opportunistic placement. From the 14th c: one deliberately conspicuous grand *vis*, and the invention goes into two places only — «Les architectes déployaient les ressources de leur imagination dans les voûtes qui les terminaient et dans la composition des noyaux». Everything else stays plain.

**Timber work: three types, one hard part rule, and thin evidence.** «Il y a les escaliers à limons droits avec poteaux, les escaliers à noyaux et les escaliers à vis sans noyaux et à limons spirales. Les marches, dans les escaliers en bois du moyen âge, sont toujours pleines, assemblées dans le limon à tenons et mortaises.» No riser boards, no open strings, no housed-and-wedged construction. The log conversion is a complete manufacturing recipe with reasons: a 0.50 m oak log, three saw cuts, six triangles, one step each, front toward the heart «étant la partie qui fatigue le plus», sapwood and wane to the tail, cuts along the direction of the shakes so the step cannot warp — directly usable for procedural grain orientation. **But** Viollet states outright «Des escaliers de bois antérieurs au XVIe siècle, il ne nous reste que très-peu de fragments», and the timber section leans on Mathurin Jousse (1627) read backwards. ### Where Viollet AGREES and DISAGREES with the books we have already closed

**Brandon, *Open Timber Roofs of the Middle Ages* (1849).**

*Real disagreement, and it changes the algorithm.* **TIE_MODE.** Brandon presents the English tie-beam and hammer-beam roofs as the achievement of medieval carpentry. Viollet says English carpenters misunderstood the tie: «Quand, par exception, les Anglais ont armé les fermes de leurs charpentes d'entraits, il semble qu'ils n'aient pas compris la fonction de cette pièce […] l'entrait porte le poinçon (désigné sous le nom de poteau royal) et par suite toute la ferme […] Il nous serait difficile de découvrir les motifs de cette ignorance d'un principe simple et connu de toute antiquité. Peut-être cela tient-il seulement à la facilité avec laquelle, dans ces contrées, on se procurait des bois d'un énorme équarrissage.» Both descriptions of the fabric agree; what differs is whether a tie may be a bearing. This is a difference of engineering doctrine, and it resolves into a clean generator switch: `TIE_MODE = {BEARING (English: the tie carries the king post, is huge and «posée sur son fort»), SUSPENDED (French: the tie carries nothing, hangs from the king post by moises pendantes, and may be slender)}`. That one switch changes the tie's section, the mid-span joint set, and the visible logic of the whole truss. Verification found this the *soundest* regional discriminator in the article — soundest because pitch, which the reader preferred, turned out not to discriminate at all.

*Real disagreement.* **Truss spacing is not a function of roof type.** Brandon's bands are non-overlapping (common-rafter 0.36 m; hammer-beam 1.68-2.39 m; tie-beam over a nave 3.35-3.81 m). Viollet cuts straight across them: Westminster Hall, the hammer-beam roof *par excellence*, spaces its *fermes* at «5 m,75 d'axe en axe», more than double Brandon's hammer-beam maximum; Mauvesin at 4.30 m; Messina, with identical trusses throughout, at «2 m,50 d'axe en axe» over a 14 m span. Brandon's bands describe fifteen small English parish roofs, not the type. Reformulated rule: if every chevron is armed, spacing may reach 0.5-0.6 of the span; if the chevrons are plain and rely on purlins, spacing is set by the purlin's safe span.

*Real disagreement, small and diagnostic.* **Rafter aspect ratio.** Brandon's common rafters are near-invariant at 0.14-0.20 × 0.076-0.114 m — slabby, because they only span between purlins in one plane. Viollet's are nearly square: Auxerre «les chevrons 0,13. sur 0,12», Sully «0,20 c. sur 0,16 c. d'équarrissage posés sur champ». The depths agree almost exactly; the thicknesses do not. Reason: a *chevron portant ferme* takes load in two planes and receives mortises from jambettes, collars and contre-fiches on its side faces. So depth/thickness ≈ 1.8-2.3 diagnoses an English purlin roof and ≈ 1.1-1.25 a French chevron-portant-ferme roof.

*Range extension rather than contradiction.* Brandon's spans cluster at 3.35-3.61 m (aisles) and 5.18-7.01 m (naves); Viollet gives Reims 14.40 m, Messina 14.00 m and Westminster 21.00 m in the clear, while his small examples (Auxerre 4.80, Mauvesin 7.00, Châlons 7-8) sit exactly inside Brandon's nave band. Treat Brandon's clusters as the PARISH band and Viollet's as the CATHEDRAL/GREAT-HALL band. Members and part counts change qualitatively above about 10 m.

*Same principle, different member.* Brandon makes the cornice the alignment member. In France it is the **double sablière**, fixed by the tie dovetailed into both plates and the blochet notched over both: «on pose deux sablières B B′ plutôt sur leur plat que carrées. C est l'entrait de la ferme-maîtresse assemblé à queues d'arondes dans les deux sablières». Where a timber cornice exists (Ely) it *masks* the plates. Treat the plate assembly as the datum object and the cornice as cladding on it.

*Agreement, plus something Brandon does not give.* Both say exposed roofs were painted. Viollet adds a **value hierarchy** usable directly: at Messina the coffered panel is «azur clair et de tons blancs et roses sur des fonds d'or» while «Les arbalétriers et les chevronnages ont admis des tons plus sombres, comme pour faire ressortir davantage l'éclat» — structure darker, panel lighter and gilded. Combined with Brandon's spiral-band motif we have both halves. Named French survivals to chase for actual patterns: the ducal palace at Dijon and Sainte-Madeleine at Châteaudun.

*The gap Brandon left, now closed — but not as the reader closed it.* Brandon's single pitch datum (100° at the ridge = 40° pitch) is one point in the shallow band, not the medieval pitch. Viollet supplies the rules and the causal driver. But see the refutation above: this does **not** yield a France/England discriminator.

**Ungewitter / Mohrmann, *Manual of Gothic Construction* (Ricker tr. 1920).**

*Real disagreement, and the headline one — it survived verification.* **Which arch commands the web coursing.** Ungewitter, p. 158: "The plane of the joints is perpendicular to the vertical plane of the diagonal groin and passes through the middle of the latter. In the diagonal section the joint plane therefore appears as a radial straight line," and on site "it is only necessary to tell a skilled mason that the courses are to be perpendicular to the diagonal arches." Viollet: «L'arc formeret doit commander d'abord» and «Ce n'est pas sans raison que l'on doit placer les cerces dans un plan vertical, et faire passer par conséquent l'arête du lit de chaque rangée de moellon dans ce plan vertical.» That is a genuine geometric divergence of roughly 45° in plan, and it needs two presets, selected by region and material, that produce visibly different cell surfaces — radial-bedded ashlar against fanned lens-shaped rubble courses. **Do not average them.**

Two corrections to how we frame it. (a) "The exact inverse of the reference" is wrong: Viollet divides **both** arcs — «on divise en nombres égaux l'extrados des arcs ogives» — only the *count* comes from the formeret. It is a change of datum arc, not of which arcs are divided. (b) Ungewitter does not state the radial rule as a law: it is item 3 of an enumerated list of coursing positions (horizontal courses, Fig. 298; perpendicular to the angle bisector for fan and net vaults, Fig. 314; then "3. Courses with bed planes perpendicular to the diagonal groins (Fig. 315) are particularly favorable for ordinary square or nearly square vaults"), and he captions it with his own regional caveat: "This position of the course is now the usual one in Germany." So the regional-preset conclusion is supported by **both** sources, not imposed on one.

*A claimed disagreement that DISSOLVED — and the agreement is worth more.* The reader called the drawable-vs-procedural axis "the deepest difference between the two sources", casting Ungewitter's web as a solvable surface against Viollet's un-drawable procedural one. Ungewitter says the opposite of what was attributed to him, twice on the same pages: p. 158, "Although the execution is not after such a drawing but is done according to the eye of a skilled master, and consequently there are always shown slight variations and irregularities, it follows the principle seen here"; p. 159, "The drawing of such curved beds will not be treated further, since it is a simple problem in descriptive geometry and is useless in practice. […] In case accurately cut stone does not come in question, such projections of courses are mostly superfluous"; p. 157, "men mostly omit its use and turn the courses entirely freehand." That is Viollet's own two-part doctrine — «Cette épure ne se fait pas sur le chantier» plus «le maçon […] n'a pas besoin d'en savoir davantage pour construire, sans épure, tout le triangle de remplissage». **Both sources say the web epure exists only to demonstrate the principle and that the mason works freehand by eye from a single verbal rule.** An independent cross-source agreement on procedural, tolerance-absorbing execution is a firmer basis for our swept-course-with-arc-length-correction implementation than the invented contradiction was.

*Convergence across two independent rationales — treat as a hard rule.* Both end up with **horizontal beds at the springing and radial beds above it**. Ungewitter gets there from the kern rule and a sliding criterion; Viollet gets there from bearing for the superposed pier and a rigid seat for the flyer head, and explicitly refuses to compute: «Définir ces actions par des calculs serait un travail en pure perte […] Mais toujours le sentiment humain, lorsqu'il est aiguisé, est plus subtil que le calcul.» Agreement reached from unrelated arguments is stronger than either alone.

*Complementary, so compose them.* Buttresses: Ungewitter gives the **profile** (depth at top = 5/11 of depth at bottom, height measured to the vault keystone); Viollet gives the **footprint** and its dependence on arch sharpness (the 16th-c construction, ≈ span/4 for a semicircle, thinning as the arch sharpens, valid only for piers ≤ 1.5 × span). Compose: `thickness_bottom = f(span, sharpness)` per Viollet, `thickness_top = 5/11 × thickness_bottom` per Ungewitter — and note Viollet's rule is only good for squat piers, exactly where Ungewitter's keystone-height rule is not needed. Ridge line: Ungewitter gives a qualitative guard ("curvature must not be carried too far"); Viollet gives an actual rise, 0.50 m, as an explicit **example** («Le constructeur dit, par exemple»), and makes the arc a *tool* that generates every course by chord-and-rise lookup. Flatness/thickness: Ungewitter tabulates wall thickness against rise; Viollet states only the principle — «plus l'arc se rapproche, dans son développement, de la ligne horizontale, plus cette poussée s'éloigne de la verticale» — and the two are consistent with each other and with the fig. 32 bis result, a three-way cross-check. Glazing allowance: Ungewitter sizes abutments assuming half the wall is opening; Viollet answers topologically instead, advancing the flyer springing outward to manufacture abutment depth where no solid culée could stand — visually distinctive and recognisable in real chevets.

*Real disagreement where Ungewitter is right.* Masonry in tension. Ungewitter: it is "always risky to count on their unbroken resistance." Viollet repeatedly counts on it — «En supposant les pierres CD d'un seul morceau chacune, résistantes et engagées à la queue sous le contre-fort, cette construction sera plus solide que si nous avions élevé une pile de fond EA sous les sommiers de la voûte», and «Ceux-ci avaient encore un avantage : ils détruisaient en partie l'effet des poussées.» The corbel-stronger-than-pier claim needs bending resistance in one stone, and "corbelling destroys thrust" is simply wrong — corbelling relocates the resultant. **Model the geometry he describes, because the geometry is what survives in real buildings; never propagate the mechanism.** His *en-délit*-as-strut reading is more defensible: an unjointed monolith really is stiffer in compression than a jointed stack, which is a stiffness argument, not a tension argument.

*Viollet unambiguously right, and directly useful.* Ribs and web are two separate systems built in two operations, and the same skeleton carries either surface: «sur deux arcs ogives et quatre formerets de mêmes dimension et figure, les rangs des remplissages en moellon sont bandés conformément au tracé H […] en France le remplissage dérive évidemment de la voûte d'arête romaine, tandis qu'en Angleterre il dérive de la coupole.» VERIFIED on fig. 65. `rib_layout` and `web_coursing_rule` are independent fields; keep fig. 65 as the reference image.

**Dollman & Jobbins, *Analysis of Ancient Domestic Architecture* (1861-63).**

*Real and load-bearing disagreement.* Their seven-step room-accretion ladder (hall, solar, parlour and bedchamber, private dining room, withdrawing room, study and boudoir, tiring rooms) is a horizontal accretion tree. Viollet's French urban rule is **vertical repetition** of one room type with a per-storey occupancy assignment: «Toujours la salle à chaque étage». The English ladder does appear in France, but only in the *hôtel*, and there as apartments with *cabinets* and *garde-robes* rather than as seven named stages. This changes the plan algorithm entirely.

*Different solution to the same problem.* No screens passage exists in the French town house. Its functional replacement is the *allée*: «une allée contenant un degré droit flanque la boutique et donne entrée directement de la rue dans la salle du premier étage», continuing to a court from which a detached kitchen is reached under cover by a gallery. In the *hôtel*, service segregation is bought with **stairs, not corridors** — fig. 34 has eight lettered stairs on one floor.

*Absence, which is itself a finding.* No solar cross-wing and no hall-versus-solar ridge hierarchy. The French equivalent of the solar is the *salle* raised over the shop, inside the same block. The height hierarchy Viollet does state is main block versus service range, expressed in storey count: «Le batiment des cuisines, les communs et le portique R n'avaient qu'un rez-de-chaussée.» Do not reuse the English solar massing for a French town. Note the smoke solutions too: a masonry flue on a party wall, a flue corbelled out over the street front (12th-early 13th c), or in rural Normandy a timber flue through the shingles — never a gable vent.

*Scope correction.* The double-height open hall with a central hearth is an English and Norman-**rural** trait, not a French urban one. The single French case that matches it is rural Normandy: «Ces maisons normandes des XIe et XIIe siècles ne contenaient qu'une salle assez élevée, éclairée de tous côtés, couverte par une charpente grossièrement lambrissée. Le foyer était placé vers le milieu de la pièce» — i.e. exactly the region that supplied England. In a French town, halls get ceilings on exposed beams and joists, and hearths go on the party walls or in the front wall.

*Methodological disagreement, and the more useful side.* They hand us a proportional canon (Penshurst 64′0″ × 38′8″, 48′0″ to the ridge, height:length exactly 3:4). Viollet offers no canon at all: dimensions come from the plot and from local lordship custom, and he attacks proportional reasoning as academic. A generator built on his derivation rule produces asymmetric elevations, and that asymmetry is correct output, not a bug.

*Agreement worth recording.* Their English parish naves at 17-23 ft (5.2-7.0 m) and Viollet's French town-house frontages fall in the same band. After correction the French figures are 4.3 m (Laval) to 9.7 m (Burgundian broad-front), so the "one nave wide" rule of thumb transfers, and 6-8 m is a defensible default frontage. What varies wildly is depth: 5.9 m for the Burgundian broad-front type, 18.4 m at Cluny, 20.2 m for a Montpazier burgage plot.

*A finding about both sources at once.* Their manors are one-off and irregular by implication; the bastides are documented regularity — planned towns of 1260-1330 with plans drawn to the line and equal lotting. So regularity is available to us for the 13th c and is evidence, not conjecture — provided we honour the correction above (identical plans, varied façades) and note that the credit to Edward I is Verneilh's, not Viollet's.

*Answers our question in the negative, which is still an answer.* On party walls and fire: no general fire code, only per-lordship custom over siting, projection, light and drainage. Fire is handled by fabric and layout instead — masonry party walls carrying the flues, the kitchen detached across a court — and «les incendies fréquents qui détruisirent des villes tout entières» are simply reported as the cost.

**Gardner, *Ironwork* — see the Serrurerie section.** Net result: agreement on no rolled stock (record as concordance, not disagreement); agreement on the red backing layer, with Viollet extending it to skin and felt and to "most hinge straps" and asserting surviving traces; one minor divergence (painted red versus dyed scarlet); and **silence, not contradiction, on the tinned finish**.

### Viollet's conjectures, and how far to trust him

He is opinionated and sometimes reconstructs rather than records. Verification found the readers' single most consistent failure was **under-flagging** this: every one of his own explicit hedge markers — *nous ne l'affirmerons pas*, *hypothèse*, *restitution*, *C'est possible*, *il est probable*, *nous supposons*, *peut-être* — occurs in the mined articles, and none appeared in the delivered files. **Process fix for the next pass: grep the source for that hedge vocabulary before setting any confidence flag.**

**Where he is at his best — first-hand observation of fabric he had open.** His criticism of the Notre-Dame choir roof («quand on enlève la volige, on fait remuer à la main les fermes-maîtresses») is a man reporting what he did with his own hands, and since that frame burned in 2019 his plates are now primary evidence for a lost structure. Ditto the Chalons details he lists (wedge, flyers, corbelled pier), all VERIFIED present on the plate.

**Where he refuses to speculate, and says so — worth quoting because it sets the evidential floor.** «Les petites dimensions des charpentes romanes encore existantes et leur extrême rareté ne nous permettent pas de nous étendre sur l'art de la charpenterie à cette époque reculée ; nous serions obligés de nous lancer dans les conjectures, et c'est ce que nous voulons éviter.» So everything from the 13th c on in *Charpente* comes from surviving fabric; the pre-1200 story is argument. Our Romanesque roof assets are less well founded than our Gothic ones. Similarly he admits a gap at Westminster: «nous n'avons pu savoir s'ils sont d'un seul morceau».

**Explicit self-labelled reconstructions — usable as design templates, never as dimensional authority.**
- The Louvre grande vis (figs 10-12): «Nous ne prétendons pas […] présenter ces figures comme un relevé scrupuleux de ce monument détruit depuis le XVIIe siècle, et dont il ne reste aucun dessin ; nous essayons ici de résumer dans une étude les diverses combinaisons employées par les architectes des XIVe et XVe siècles.» Honest, and it must be carried forward. The step length he drew (2.27 m) matches Sauval's seven *pieds* exactly, so he worked carefully from the documents — but his landing sequence (16+16+16+35) does not close against the ~36 winders per revolution he actually drew.
- The Bernardins double vis (fig. 13): his own attempt to make a figure out of Sauval's verbal description.
- Amiens fig. 9 bis: «Nous avons rétabli le pignon et le rez-de-chaussée d'après d'autres fragments du même temps et des mêmes contrées» — only the two arcatured upper storeys are surviving fabric.
- Cluny fig. 5: the façade survives only to level C, so the crowning timber loggia is a restitution, and «quant aux bâtiments postérieurs, il en reste à peine quelques traces».
- Maison fig. 3 is a **composite**, footnoted «D'après des plans recueillis particulièrement en Bourgogne, dans le Nivernais et la haute Champagne» — ideal as a generator template, useless as evidence for any one house. Maison figs 1 and 2 are houses carved on capitals (Vézelay, Moissac): conventionalised images at two removes, used because «De ces habitations antérieures au XIe siècle, il ne reste rien aujourd'hui».
- The Sens vault *tracé*: «nous n'établissons notre tracé que sur une hypothèse», then «voici qui vient encore appuyer notre restitution».

**Conjectures the readers reported as fact, now reclassified.**
- **The rib genealogy** (*Voûtes* finding 0, the showcase finding): flip to conjecture and carry his disclaimer, «Que les architectes occidentaux aient fait ce raisonnement en plein XIIe siècle, nous ne l'affirmerons pas».
- **Liernes and tiercerons derived functionally**: the derivation sits inside a frame he marks as speculation — «Les Anglais ont-ils fait des voûtes originairement composées d'arcs de pierre ou de courbes de bois, sur lesquelles ils auraient posé des madriers, des couchis, en un mot ? **C'est possible**» — and the same article says «on ne doit repousser absolument aucune conjecture». Downgrade from "a generator can emit tiercerons by rule" to "a plausible placement heuristic".
- **The Sens coursing**: he withdraws it himself, «nous ne pouvons affirmer toutefois que les rangs de moellons de ces triangles aient été posés parallèlement à la ligne des clefs».
- **Motive attribution generally.** The flag policy was internally inconsistent: identical constructions were flagged conjecture in some places and fact in others. All of these impute intent with no cited source and should be split into `observed_geometry` versus `attributed_motive`: «Ces constructeurs avaient trop de sens pour imaginer les crossettes I»; «L'idée de surélever les pleins cintres […] n'était donc pas un caprice […] mais le résultat d'un calcul bien simple de constructeur»; «on reconnut […] l'avantage qu'il y aurait […] 1o pour […] 2o pour laisser passer les entraits»; «on banda un nerf en pierre BF **pour** recevoir les extrémités […] et cacher les sutures». The geometry in each stays reportable; the motive is his inference. A single boolean forces a choice and the reader kept choosing "reported".
- **The Norman-origin thesis for Anglo-Norman carpentry**, argued from ship carpentry while conceding the French examples are all gone. Useful as a stylistic driver (compass timber, clamped assemblies, no tie); not a documented fact.
- **The medieval date of the pivoting stair**, inferred from absence: «Nous avons quelquefois trouvé des cages cylindriques en maçonnerie dans des châteaux, avec des portes à chaque étage, sans aucune trace d'escalier de pierre ou de bois ; **il est probable** que ces cages renfermaient des escaliers de ce genre, et **nous pensons** que cette invention est fort ancienne.» Use the mechanism; do not date it to the Middle Ages on this authority. He knows it from a 1627 book.
- **The Chalons anticipated-settlement narrative** — his most extended and least evidenced intentional reading, and he concedes it: «Tout ceci peut être subtil, trop subtil, nous l'accordons.» Model the geometry; discard the intent.
- **Unsourced survival statistics**: «toutes les nefs élevées sur des colonnes monocylindriques […] se sont rarement déformées» against «la plupart de celles où les piles […] montent de fond, se sont courbées» — no building named, no count given. Historically interesting, evidentially thin.

**Where he is a restorer writing about his own work, and it matters.** The centreless web method is validated by his own 19th-century sites: «nous n'avons jamais éprouvé de difficulté à faire adopter cette méthode dans la pratique.» That is a plausibility argument, not evidence that 12th-century masons worked that way. Use the method as a generator; do not cite it as documented medieval fact. His restoration hand also touches the evidence directly: of the two Sainte-Chapelle *sacraire* stairs, only one is ancient — the second was remade on the model of the survivor during his own works.

**Where his framework must simply be discarded.** *Maison* repeatedly attributes construction systems to "races" («l'art de la charpente… n'appartient qu'aux races indo-germaniques»). This is pseudo-science and carries no evidential weight. Keep the technical observations it is wrapped around — log-stacking versus assembled framing, fir versus oak, the four provincial carpentry systems — which stand on their own and which he also justifies materially: «S'il est difficile, en effet, d'empiler horizontalement des brins de chêne qui demandent un équarrissement long et pénible».

**Where his polemic contradicts his own evidence.** Two cases, and in both prefer the evidence. (1) He denies that any medieval municipality imposed uniformity — «Aucune municipalité alors n'aurait songé à imposer à tous les propriétaires d'une même rue une hauteur uniforme de bandeaux et un style uniforme d'architecture» — and then documents the bastides in detail. (2) He denies that a stair was ever theatre — «L'idée de faire d'un escalier une façon de décoration théâtrale […] cette idée n'était jamais entrée dans l'esprit d'un architecte» — and then describes ten canopied statues, lanterns and terraces, and gives Raymond du Temple's first stated reason for the Louvre siting as making it «plus visible et plus aisé à trouver». The tension resolves on the date: pre-14th c functional, 14th c onward one deliberately conspicuous grand *vis*.

**Reading traps that will bite the next pass.**
- «0,20 c.» means **0.20 metres**; the `c.` is a printer's tail, not centimetres. Proof internal to the text: «huit pouces (0,22 c.)» and «un pied […] (0,33 c.)». At one point he writes «un octogone de 0,13 centimètres de diamètre», which must mean 0.13 m. Apply this or every dimension is out by a factor of 100.

- The wikisource transcription itself has at least one error: «conformément à l'épure tracée fig. 50» must mean fig. 55.
- Commons rate-limits hard. `fetch_image` does not validate magic bytes, and failed fetches come back as 2141-2142 byte HTML files with a `.png` name — 18 of 32 "successful" escalier downloads were HTML on the first pass. Check the PNG signature, pace at ≥3 s, and note that one legitimate plate (Voûtes fig. 56) really is only 3540 bytes, so size alone is not a test.

### V — charpente: every pitch rule and every dimension claimed, and that each French quote is real and correctly translated

**Why refuted.** Quotes and numbers hold up almost completely — but the single most load-bearing PITCH RULE in the report is wrong, and it is contradicted by the reader's own verified figures.

1. REFUTED — the regional pitch bands (finding 2, and disagreement 5's generator rule "pitch(French Gothic) = 60-65; pitch(Anglo-Norman great hall) about 53", sold as "a usable REGION/DATE discriminator... exactly what we lacked").
 (a) Viollet says the opposite of a France/England split. Verbatim: "Excepté dans les provinces méridionales, où les charpentes conservèrent une faible inclinaison, partout en France et en Angleterre, on modifia, à la fin du XIIe siècle, le système des charpentes apparentes" — "Except in the southern provinces, where roofs kept a shallow pitch, EVERYWHERE IN FRANCE AND IN ENGLAND the system of exposed roofs was modified at the end of the 12th century." The low-pitch exception Viollet names is intra-French (the Midi); England is grouped WITH France in the steepening. The reader quotes this very sentence in finding 12 and then contradicts it in finding 2.
 (b) The reader's own VERIFIED fig 31 is Ely cathedral — "La nef et les transsepts de la cathédrale d'Ély sont encore couverts par une belle charpente qui date de la fin du XIVe siècle" — i.e. an ENGLISH late-14th-c roof. The reader's fig-31 entry is the ONE verified figure for which it recorded no pitch — the sole English counter-datum is the one omitted.
 (c) "Everything French measured or computed here sits at 57-68 degrees" is false against the reader's own data. Its VERIFIED fig 25 (Saint-Jean de Châlons-sur-Marne, French, 13th c, one of the "7 m 00 à 8 m 00" apparent roofs) it measures at ~50 degrees; I measure 49.8 degrees. Its own figs 1, 2 and 5 (French) read 41, 42 and 39 degrees.

2. REFUTED — misquote with the scope stripped out, promoted to a generator invariant. Figure 0's modelling_notes present as Viollet's words: "la flexion des arbalétriers est arrêtée à intervalles égaux par les trois entraits retroussés". The article reads: "La flexion des arbalétriers DE CETTE FERME est donc arrêtée à intervalles égaux par les trois entraits retroussés, rendus rigides eux-mêmes par les sous-arbalétriers." The unmarked elision deletes exactly the words ("de cette ferme") that limit the statement to the Notre-Dame hip truss, and the reader then hard-codes it as invariant "collars are spaced at EQUAL intervals up the rafter (buckling length, not composition)". Viollet is describing one truss, not stating a rule. This is the only French string in the whole file that is not verbatim.

3. INCOMPLETE — finding 0 calls the bahut-width account "THE pitch rule" and "the stated reason". Viollet states a second, independent cause earlier and at a different date (mid-12th c): "employant des bois moins gros, il fallut donner aux arbalétriers une plus grande inclinaison, afin qu'ils ne fléchissent pas sous le poids de la couverture" — "using smaller timbers, it became necessary to give the principal rafters a greater inclination so that they would not bend under the weight of the covering." The reader's recommendation ("implement pitch as a function of available wall-head width and covering type") drops the scantling driver Viollet explicitly gives, and collapses two dated changes into one.

4. FABRICATED DERIVATION on a real number. Dimension #4's note: "0.20 + 0.20 + 0.12 stacked outside the rafter line plus bearing = 1.10 m of wall." Those figures sum to 0.52, and Viollet gives no such sum. His stated cause is shallow pitch: "si le comble n'a pas une forte inclinaison et si l'on veut que la rencontre des arbalétriers avec l'entrait ne porte pas à faux, ce système exige des murs d'une grande épaisseur." The 1.10 m is a geometric consequence of low pitch, not an addition of scantlings — which matters, because the reader's own pitch story depends on that coupling.

Dimension #36's note calls 5.40 m "the threshold above which two stacked courbes are needed"; the text says only that THIS truss has 5.40 m span ("Cette ferme n'a que 5 m,40 de portée") and that for greater spans one-piece courbes were hard to find — no threshold is stated. **What the record should say.** Replace finding 2 and the pitch half of disagreement 5 with what the article actually supports.

WHAT VIOLLET STATES (keep):
- Pre-change pitch 40 or 50 degrees, raised to 60 and even 65: "on la porta de 40 ou 50 degrés à 60 et même 65 degrés."
- 45 to 50 degrees for the rafters of the failed trusses set over Burgundian pointed barrels (Autun, Beaune, Saulieu are the buildings named): "les arbalétriers, n'ayant guère qu'une inclinaison de 45 à 50 degrés."
- The change is dated to the end of the 12th c and applies to FRANCE AND ENGLAND ALIKE; the one low-pitch exception is the southern French provinces: "Excepté dans les provinces méridionales... partout en France et en Angleterre."
- TWO stated causes, at two dates, not one: (i) mid-12th c, smaller scantlings force a steeper pitch so the rafters do not bend under the covering; (ii) 13th c, the bahut has shrunk to about 1 m and the rafter foot plus purlin depth no longer fits on it.
- Covering material caps pitch: tuile romaine implies a near-flat roof; a steep Romanesque gable implies grandes tuiles plates.

The biggest win is the one we came for: **Viollet gives pitch rules, and gives the reason.** Pitch was raised from 40–50° to "60 et même 65 degrés" at the start of the 13th c *because the bahut (wall head over the vaults) had shrunk to about 1 m and the rafter foot no longer fitted on it*. So pitch is derivable from wall-head width and covering type, not a style constant. Reims's stated triangle (14.40 m base × 15.50 m rise, rafters 17.00 m) computes to 65.1° and the rafter length checks to 0.5% — the numbers are internally consistent.
