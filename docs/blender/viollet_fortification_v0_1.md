### The arms race, feature by feature

Viollet organises all four articles round a single narrative: attack and defence alternate the lead, and every form in the group is dated by which of them was ahead. This is what turns a castle from a look into a dated parameter set.

**Roman and Gallo-Roman (to the 5th century).** Curtain flanked by towers on the Vitruvian rule; twin carriage bays and twin pedestrian bays at the gate; **no portcullis and no moving bridge at all** — "Les baies, s'ouvrant sur la voie publique, n'étaient fermées que par des vantaux de menuiserie, sans herses ni ponts mobiles" (the gate bays, opening on the public road, were closed only by joinery leaves, without portcullises or moving bridges). Wall built as two facings twenty *pieds* apart with the ditch spoil rammed between. Gate towers at Arles are 8 m across and 15 m apart. Isolated signal towers on mounds already have their door raised above ground.

**5th to 10th century.** Hasty refortification out of demolished monuments; much of it timber. Viollet is explicit that no system can be recovered here: "il est probable qu'elles n'étaient point soumises à des dispositions régulières et systématiques".

**c.1040 — the Norman donjon.** Arques: rectangular, unvaulted, timber floors on internal corbels, **door raised six metres** and reached only by ladder — "Sa porte est relevée à six mètres au-dessus du sol". The raised door is the defining early feature and it is also the type's fatal flaw, as Château-Gaillard proved.

**12th century — the gate becomes the target.** Because nothing but the sap, the ram and the mine could touch a thick wall, every assault went at the doors, and the doors shrank to match: "elles ont à peine 3 mètres d'ouverture sur 3 à 4 mètres de hauteur sous clef". The Carcassonne château gate of c.1120 is already the full grammar — a removable bridge deck over a 3 m by 3 m pit, a broad machicolation, portcullis, leaf, second portcullis — in an opening under 2 m wide by 2.30 m high.

**c.1200 — Coucy, and the gate as an independent fort.** At the porte de Laon the two portcullis winches are geared so that **only one can ever be raised at a time**, and the guard-room doors are turned so the passage cannot see how many men are inside. Viollet is blunt about why: mercenary garrisons sold places, "beaucoup de places étaient prises par la trahison d'un poste". Anti-treachery, not anti-army, drives half the detail of this period.

**Mid 13th century — Saint Louis, the double enceinte and the *lices*.** Two concentric circuits with the ward between them palisaded into compartments. Outer towers are **50 to 60 m apart**, low, mostly closed at the gorge, and built for distant fire; inner towers are closer together, project less, and give "courts flanquements très-multipliés". Barbicans stop being earth and timber and become masonry, always open at the gorge so they cannot be held against the place.

**1285 — the round tower's dead point is admitted.** Spurs (*becs*, *éperons*) are added at the tangent: "On avait donc reconnu dès le XIIIe siècle l'inconvénient des tours rondes, leur faiblesse au point de la tangente parallèle aux courtines." They force the attacker off the tangent and destroy the ram's purchase.

**End of the 13th century — defence wins.** "À la fin du XIIIe siècle, la défense des places avait acquis une supériorité évidente sur l'attaque", and a well-found place can only be taken by close blockade.

**Early 14th century — timber goes, stone machicolation comes.** Engines learned in the crusades set fire to hoardings however well hidden and soaked: "On renonça donc d'abord aux hourds de bois mobiles… et on les remplaça par des hourds de pierre, des mâchicoulis." Mining improves at the same time, so the gate stops being the decisive point, **and gates widen** — the 14th-century norm becomes 3.50 m by 3.50 m, the chicanes in the passage disappear, and effort moves outward into flanking and outworks.

**Mid to late 14th century — powder arrives quietly.** Guns are iron tubes throwing two- or three-pound balls, or round pebbles; they replace the great crossbows and are mounted behind the tower merlons. Because flat-trajectory fire is still weak, the answer is to **raise the towers further**: "plus on l'élevait, plus il pouvait causer de dommages aux assiégeants". Pierrefonds, finished about 1400, is the last complete statement of the pre-artillery system.

**1428–1450 — the Bureau brothers and the collapse.** Orléans' defenders already have cannon shooting point-blank to 600 m. With trench approaches and massed guns, towns that took the English four months (Harfleur, 1440) fall in weeks.

**Late 15th to 16th century — the medieval crown is dismantled.** Barbicans are razed and earthed for barbette batteries; *fausses braies* replace the *lices*; the old walls are backed with earth inside. Two facts kill the medieval profile outright: revetments of only 0.30–0.50 m spall off the core under iron shot, and **the merlons themselves become the danger** — "les merlons de pierre enlevés par les boulets se brisaient en éclats, véritable mitraille plus meurtrière encore que les boulets eux-mêmes". Walls thicken (7.00 m at Langres), command is lowered, and by the middle of the 16th century the bastion is general.

### Parameter set: curtain

| element | value | governed by |
|---|---|---|
| thickness, pre-gunpowder | 2–3 m | resistance to ram, sap and mine |
| wall walk width | ≤ 2 m | enough for shooters, too little for guns — the reason 15th-century walls need internal earth ramps |
| stone parapet thickness | 0.50–0.70 m | pierced with closely set archères at a near-right opening angle |
| ashlar facing over rubble core | 0.30–0.50 m | adequate against stone shot, fatal against iron |
| **outer** enceinte height, ditch bottom or scarp crest to hoarding floor | ~10 m | anti-escalade minimum |
| **inner** enceinte height, *lices* floor to hoarding floor | ≥ 14 m | anti-escalade minimum, plus command over the outer circuit |

The height figure is the important generator rule and Viollet states it as a rule: the two enceintes each hold *a minimum height along their whole development* to be escalade-proof, and where the ground rises and falls the wall follows it so that the height above outside ground stays constant — "les remparts se conforment aux mouvements du sol, et les hourds suivent l'inclinaison du chemin de ronde". A generator should therefore drive wall-head level from terrain plus a constant, not from a fixed datum. He then states the general claim outright: "Il y avait donc alors des données, des règles, des formules pour l'architecture militaire."

Crown, when timber (*hourds*): stone parapet kept standing behind the hoarding, so a burnt hoarding still leaves a defence. Crown, when stone: corbels stacked in courses at **0.70–1.20 m axis to axis**, carrying a crenellated wall **0.33–0.40 m thick and 2 m high**, with a timber *coursière* behind for circulation and resupply. On towers the same storey gains a second, covered crenellated storey above it.

### Parameter set: tower

Spacing is the one interval Viollet gives a stated cause for, and he gives it through Vitruvius: "L'intervalle entre les tours doit être calculé en raison de la portée d'un trait" — the interval is a function of shot range, so that anyone at the curtain is taken from both flanks. Everything else follows from it. Observed values: 15 m between Roman gate towers of 8 m diameter; **50–60 m** on the 13th-century outer enceinte at Coucy; about **100 m** at Vincennes, which Viollet flags as *past* the old limit — "ce qui dépasse la limite des anciennes escarpes flanquées".

Diameters run 9.85 m (Montlhéry, above the talus, "30 pieds") through 15.50 m (Pierrefonds donjon towers), 16 m (La Rochelle), 20 m (Vincennes, at 5 m above ground), 22 m (Aigues-Mortes) to 30.50 m (the Coucy donjon). Wall thickness 4 m to 5.20 m ("16 pieds") at Coucy; 10 m at Ham. Three rules hold across the set:

1. **Solid through the height of the talus**, so the tower cannot be sapped: "Ces tours pleines dans la hauteur du talus qui enveloppe la roche naturelle ne pouvaient être ruinées par la sape."
2. **Asymmetric masonry** — the cylinder is thicker on the field side than on the town side; the internal chamber is set eccentrically.
3. **Command over the curtain is always kept**, even into the 16th century when it no longer made sense: "On croyait toujours devoir faire dominer les tours sur les courtines."

Plus the Vitruvian circulation rule Viollet endorses: interrupt the wall walk at each tower by a gap the width of the tower and bridge it with unfixed timber, so a captured curtain gives nothing else.

### Parameter set: gate

Sequence along the passage, in the order an attacker meets them (porte Narbonnaise, 1285): chain — machicolation — portcullis — barred leaf — flanking loophole — great central machicolation — third machicolation — second portcullis — final leaves. The passage floor **rises from outside to inside** so a defending column pushes downhill, and Viollet notes this as the admitted usage wherever the ground allowed.

| period | opening |
|---|---|
| Gallo-Roman | wide, twin bays, no portcullis or bridge |
| 11th–12th c. | ~3 m wide, 3–4 m high; Carcassonne château gate under 2 m by 2.30 m |
| late 13th c. exception | 4.20 m at Villeneuve-lez-Avignon, "largeur inusitée" |
| 14th c. norm | 3.50 m by 3.50 m |
| postern with single-arm bridge | ≤ 1 m wide, 2–2.50 m high |

Command always comes from the top — "le commandement se faisait toujours du haut" — which is why the gates carry vertical command holes (0.63 by 0.50 m at Carcassonne, big enough to pass a ladder) and small windows over the machicolations for shouting orders down to the portcullis crews.

### Parameter set: donjon

Defined not by size but by independence: it commands the castle, but it also has **its own issue to open country** — "il possède toujours une issue particulière sur la campagne" — and that is what distinguishes it from a tower. The reason given is social, not military: the lord distrusts his own vassals as much as the enemy.

Development: rectangular Norman type with raised door (Arques c.1040, walls with 0.15–0.20 m by 0.20–0.32 m facing stones); quatrefoil and lobed types (Étampes, walls not under 4 m, with a deliberate 4 m drop-trap inside the postern for anyone who runs straight in); then **cylindrical from Philip Augustus** (Louvre, 20 m diameter, ~40 m high) after Château-Gaillard fell because the garrison could not get back through a raised door in time. Coucy, the maximum: 30.50 m diameter *hors œuvre*, 55 m high from the paved ditch floor, 5.50 m of masonry behind the wall niches, a chemise with an 8 m paved ditch between it and the tower, and a well still 30 m deep unemptied.

### Mechanisms — and whether they are shown in section

### How far to trust Viollet in this group

This is the group where he is least safe, and the reason is that he is not only writing about these buildings, he is rebuilding three of them. Carcassonne, Pierrefonds and Coucy supply most of the figures and most of the numbers in this cycle, and Pierrefonds was **an active building site of his own** when he drew it: "Le donjon reprendra son ancien aspect ; déjà la partie de la tour carrée qui avait été jetée bas est remontée."

Hedge counts across the four articles (raw string matches): *devaient* 43, *devait* 33, *paraît/paraissent* 78, *supposé/supposant* 31, *peut-être* 25, *probablement* 23, *restaur\** 18, *aurait/auraient* 17, *il semble/semblerait* 9, *nous pensons* 4, *nous croyons* 3, *restitu\** 2, *il est probable* 2, *hypothèse* 1, *nous supposons* 1, *vraisemblable* 1, *données insuffisantes* 1. The important pattern is that the *hedged* claims are not the marginal ones — they are frequently the upper defensive storey, which is exactly the part a generator most wants.

**(a) Measured surviving fabric — trust it.** Coucy's donjon dimensions, the Carcassonne postern sizes and sill heights, wall and course thicknesses, corbel spacings, ditch widths, the loophole sizes, the machicolation hole at Pierrefonds. He is a working architect measuring stone, and the sheets carry metric scale bars (I verified 0–20 m on the outer-enceinte tower section, 0–15 m on the tour de Charlemagne section, 0–3 m on the postern drawbridge). 92 of the 116 rows in `dimensions.tsv` are classed measured-fabric.

**(b) Documented reconstruction — usable with the citation attached.** The Louvre donjon's 20 m by 40 m rests on nothing but text: "il ne reste que des descriptions très-laconiques et des figurés fort imparfaits ; nous savons seulement que…". The tour de Nesle comes from old plans and views, and even then its roof is a guess — "qui peut-être était primitivement couverte par un comble conique". The porte Saint-Lazare of Avignon is openly a restitution from an archivist's drawings: "les renseignements qui nous ont aidé à restituer cette porte dans son état primitif". The Roman and Gaulish rampart plates are reconstructions from Caesar and Vegetius, and the bastion plates are copied from Scala (Rome, 1596) and De Ville — foreign printed treatises, in Roman feet of 0.297896 m, not French fabric.

**(c) His own restitution or asserted principle — do not record as fact.** Three concrete examples worth naming:

1. **The Pierrefonds hoarding plate is labelled as a restoration in his own text** — "Voici la restauration (37) de cette partie des défenses de Pierrefonds" — and it sits immediately after the plate of the ruin as found (fig 36). The pair is honest; the second plate alone is not.
2. **The Rouen donjon's whole upper storey is admitted invention**: "nous n'avons, pour restaurer la partie supérieure, que des données insuffisantes. Toutefois **on doit admettre** que cette partie supérieure comprenait, suivant l'usage, un étage sans plancher et l'étage de la défense…". "One must admit" is doing all the work in that sentence.
3. **The Provins hoardings are inferred from a ledge**: "La position des hourds en bois des quatre faces supérieures **ne paraît pas douteuse** ; on ne s'expliquerait pas autrement la retraite ménagée au-dessus du chemin de ronde". A confident double negative resting on one architectural feature.

Add to those the routine caption "les hourds étant supposés mis en place pour la défense" — **every timber superstructure in this group is his**, drawn onto surviving corbel sockets. Treat hoarding geometry as a plausible reconstruction of a lost element, never as measured fabric.

Two functional assertions also deserve flagging rather than recording, because both are contested by later scholarship and both would change a generator's output:

- **That arrow loops were near-useless.** "ces ouvertures… servaient plutôt à reconnaître les mouvements des assiégeants et à donner du jour et de l'air dans les salles des tours qu'à la défense ; elles battaient les dehors suivant un angle trop aigu." That is Viollet's judgement, offered without a test, and it is the basis of his repeated claim that "la véritable défense était disposée au sommet des ouvrages." Build loops to his *dimensions*; do not build a game's ballistics on his *verdict*.
- **The garrison arithmetic** — "environ un homme par mètre de développement" for Carcassonne, and 1,200 men for Pierrefonds. Both are his estimates, not documents.

**Practical rule for the factory.** Take Viollet's dimensions of things that were standing when he measured them. Take his *reasons* seriously — they are his real strength, and a stated reason (the talus defeats the sap; the spur pushes the attacker off the tangent; the burnt hoarding leaves a parapet standing; the interval equals a bowshot) is exactly the invariant a generator must hold true when parameters move. Do not take his skylines. Anything above the last surviving course — hoardings, crenellations, roofs, pinnacles, watch turrets — is his drawing, not the building's.
